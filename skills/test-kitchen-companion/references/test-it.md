# Test it (Session 3)

Use this when someone has a prototype (a skill, a notebook, a Gem, or just a prompt) and wants to see how well it works or improve it.

## Set up the workspace first

Three windows, kept separate:

1. **The instructions**, in a Google Doc. This is the source of truth they edit.
2. **The prototype**, used in a fresh chat each time they test.
3. **A review chat** (optional, can be this one), where they think about results and plan changes.

Organizing the environment this way is one of the most durable skills from the workshop. It keeps them from losing track of which version they're testing.

## A small testing loop

1. **Expectation first.** Ask what they expect a good response to look like. Get one or two things they can actually observe ("it asks for the student's own idea before suggesting any").
2. **Pick three cases:**
   - **Normal:** a typical, well-formed request.
   - **Ambiguous:** a request missing key context ("I have a fuzzy idea").
   - **Boundary:** something outside its job, or a request that would undercut the purpose. For a learning tool, ask it to just give the answer and see whether the learning survives.
3. **Run them in a fresh chat** and look at the actual responses together. Don't predict or simulate results; they need to really run it. Paste the response here if they want help reading it.
4. **Change one thing.** Suggest the single revision most likely to fix the biggest problem.
5. **Retest** the case that failed and one that previously worked, so a fix doesn't break something else.

Have them capture each round in one line:

> I expected ___. It did ___. I changed ___. The next test showed ___. I still need to check ___.

## Widening the testing

From least to most effort:

1. **Heuristics:** the golden rule and the edge-case check ("Where would a competent, well-meaning person have to guess?").
2. **Scenarios:** realistic situations, including messy ones.
3. **Personas:** play a specific user: a confused first-year student, a skeptical colleague, someone in a hurry. You can play a persona for them if they ask, clearly labeled as role-play.
4. **Real users:** a colleague tries it. Ask them to think out loud.

Another AI's review is a perspective, not proof. Facts still need checking against a real source.

## Decide what it is: the commitment ladder

Help them place their project, and say what each rung asks of them:

1. **For me:** a skill or personal notebook. They maintain it when they feel like it.
2. **For my team or colleagues:** a shared notebook, or a skill once sharing is available. Someone needs to own updates.
3. **For students in my course:** usually a shared notebook. Tell students it's a prototype. The creator won't see their conversations.
4. **A tool people depend on** (hosted apps, coding tools, anything storing data): outside the Test Kitchen's scope. Who maintains it at 11pm? What data does it touch? Point them to the facilitators to talk about next steps; don't promise a path.

The step from "works for me" to "others depend on it" is where onboarding, access, support, and data questions show up. That's worth naming without discouraging them.

## Wrap-up questions

- "What surprised you while testing?"
- "What would you want to build next that you can't do with the tools you have?" (Encourage them to share the answer with the facilitators; it helps shape what BC offers.)
- "What's the next small experiment?"
